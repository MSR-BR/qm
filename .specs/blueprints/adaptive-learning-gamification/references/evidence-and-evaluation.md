# Academic evidence and evaluation

## Evidence standard

Treat gamification as a layer around an instructional design, not as the instructional design itself. A point, badge, leaderboard, timer, or streak is justified only when its proposed mechanism, learning behavior, evidence, risk, and evaluation measure are explicit.

Use this evidence order:

1. replicated learning-science findings and high-quality syntheses;
2. controlled studies that measure learning, retention, or transfer;
3. controlled or quasi-experimental studies that measure motivation, engagement, or classroom climate;
4. implementation reports and learner attitudes;
5. metadata-only evidence maps as discovery aids.

Do not promote a lower-tier result into a higher-tier claim. More time, attempts, clicks, satisfaction, or motivation can be useful, but none proves better conceptual learning.

## Outcome model

Evaluate at least four outcome families separately:

- **Learning:** concept accuracy, explanation quality, transfer to a changed representation, delayed retention, and prerequisite repair.
- **Behavior:** started and completed authentic activities, attempts, meaningful simulator interactions, and return to due practice.
- **Experience:** perceived competence, autonomy, relevance, workload, confidence, and satisfaction.
- **Equity and safety:** accessibility, differential effects, discouragement, unwanted social comparison, farming, coercion, and privacy incidents.

Never collapse these outcomes into a single `engagement` or `mastery` number.

## Mechanism card required for every game element

Before implementing a mechanic, record:

- instructional goal;
- learner behavior it is intended to support;
- psychological mechanism, such as feedback, goal clarity, competence, autonomy, relatedness, or calibrated challenge;
- observable evidence that the behavior occurred;
- learning outcome that must be measured separately;
- known risks and the opt-out or fallback path;
- implementation-fidelity event showing that the learner actually saw or used the mechanic;
- review date and policy version.

If the team cannot complete this card, do not add the mechanic.

## Physics-specific learning cycle

Prefer a short cycle that makes reasoning visible:

1. state or select a prediction before calculation or simulation;
2. identify the physical model, assumptions, representation, units, and relevant principle;
3. solve, derive, compare, or manipulate a model;
4. receive explanatory feedback linked to reviewed source material;
5. explain the physical meaning and check a limiting case, units, normalization, or probability;
6. retry a changed near-transfer item when needed;
7. retrieve the concept again after a delay and in another representation.

For simulations, a page open is exposure only. Meaningful evidence requires a prediction, a purposeful parameter change or goal, and a short comparison or reflection.

## Concept and prerequisite model

Maintain an explicit graph of reviewed concepts, skills, representations, and prerequisites. Learner state is evidence attached to graph nodes and edges, not a permanent trait label.

Knowledge-space or Bayesian-style modeling may be introduced only when the data and validation justify it. Until then, use transparent deterministic rules that:

- distinguish unaided success, hinted success, solution reveal, error, and low-confidence success;
- allow slips and guesses instead of treating one response as truth;
- represent `insufficient evidence` honestly;
- select prerequisites before repeatedly presenting an impossible target;
- support non-linear learning paths and changed problem forms.

## Evidence register supplied on 2026-09-23

### Richter and Kickmeier-Rust (2025)

`Gamification in Physics Education: Play Your Way to Better Learning`, DOI `10.17083/ijsg.v12i1.858`.

- Small crossover study of a basketball-physics game with approximately 19–20 tenth-grade learners.
- Scores, time pressure, and competition increased several engagement measures and reported motivation.
- Quiz results did not show a clear learning advantage between conditions.
- The knowledge-structure analysis supports concept/skill-level, non-linear progress rather than page-count mastery.
- Limitations include the small single-school sample, short exposure, novelty/order effects, coarse knowledge levels, and internal reporting inconsistencies in sample counts and one reported p-value.

Design consequence: model concepts and prerequisites, but do not infer learning from time, attempts, or score alone.

### Balci, Secaur, and Morris (2022)

`Comparing the effectiveness of badges and leaderboards on academic performance and motivation of students in fully versus partially gamified online physics classes`, DOI `10.1007/s10639-022-10983-z`.

- Two semester-long randomized factorial experiments in undergraduate online physics, with samples of 102 and 88.
- Badges, leaderboards, their combination, and broader versus narrower structural gamification did not improve academic performance.
- Many learners reported liking the elements or using them for monitoring, while some ignored or disliked them.
- Exposure fidelity mattered: some learners never viewed the leaderboard.
- Ceiling effects, a well-designed base course, self-report measures, static leaderboards, and a limited set of mechanics constrain generalization.

Design consequence: badges and leaderboards are optional feedback structures, not evidence of pedagogy. Never make a public individual leaderboard the default.

### Gaurina, Alajbeg, and Weber (2025)

`The Power of Play: Investigating the Effects of Gamification on Motivation and Engagement in Physics Classroom`, DOI `10.3390/educsci15010104`.

- Eight-week high-school classroom study; 69 learners were recruited and 65 appear in the reported group table.
- Structural gamification improved several classroom-perception items involving fun, satisfaction, cooperation, and competition.
- The study explicitly did not measure physics learning outcomes.
- The intervention allowed accumulated points to alter later test conditions or scores; that feature is not suitable for this blueprint because it compromises comparability and fairness.
- Limitations include small clustered groups, short duration, no baseline knowledge measure, no open-ended feedback, and sample/reporting inconsistencies.

Design consequence: collaboration and classroom climate are legitimate outcomes, but points must never buy answers, exemptions, or grades.

## Evaluation design

Before broad release, define the primary learning outcome and minimum useful effect. Prefer a staged rollout or randomized/stepped-wedge comparison when ethical and feasible.

At minimum:

- capture a baseline or prior-knowledge measure;
- record assignment, exposure, attrition, and implementation fidelity;
- use equivalent pre, post, and delayed measures when making retention claims;
- include changed representations or near-transfer items when making mastery claims;
- report sample size, missing data, effect size, uncertainty, and adverse or discouraging effects;
- analyze whether benefits differ by prior knowledge and accessibility needs without inferring fixed learner types;
- keep product analytics separate from authoritative learning evidence;
- retain a no-gamification or reduced-gamification path when practical.

Qualitative feedback may explain outcomes but cannot substitute for performance evidence. A positive attitude result should be reported as a positive attitude result.
